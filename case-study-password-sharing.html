Case Study 01 Monetization

Password Sharing

Designing the customer experience that shifted a common behavior into a new monetization model, while protecting customer trust.

Role
Executive Director, Product Design
Team
4 Product Designers, 1 Content Designer, 1 Researcher
Scope
Disney+, Hulu; Commerce, Growth, and Identity
Overview

Password Sharing was a global growth initiative across Disney+ and Hulu. Our objective was to evolve a familiar customer behavior into a sustainable monetization model without compromising trust.

I led a cross-functional design team of product designers, content design, and UX research, partnering closely with Product, Engineering, Legal, Regional teams, and executive leadership to shape the end-to-end customer experience across commerce, growth, and identity.

Research

How do people think about sharing?

Before designing a solution, I wanted to answer a fundamental question: how do subscribers actually think about sharing? I made the decision to invest in early research because I believed the wrong assumption would lead to the wrong product strategy.

Most people don't view sharing as wrong

"Even though my dad has his own house and my kids have their own place, I consider them part of my household… This is my house, but it's a family home."

Parent with Adult Children Living Elsewhere (U.S.)

The research challenged one of our core assumptions: most subscribers didn't believe they were doing anything wrong. Users weren't thinking about household as a legal or technical definition. They were thinking about the people they considered family and the relationships they were trying to preserve.

Common scenarios driving sharing behaviors

These weren't hypothetical. They were real households we interviewed. Seeing this made one thing clear: no single rule could account for all of it.

A solution built around primary residence breaks down the moment you see families actually living this way. We needed a model that could adapt rather than a fixed definition of household. That distinction shaped the strategy I asked the team to design toward.

The reframe

This wasn't a funnel problem.
It was a behavior-change problem.

Since users didn't see sharing as abuse, blunt interventions would fail, risking engagement, trust, and conversion. I defined the problem as designing an experience customers could understand and adapt to without feeling forced.

Business priorities

Convert borrowers while protecting the relationship

The business priorities were straightforward on paper: grow new subscriptions, retain paying subscribers, and monetize sharing while preserving ad-supported viewing inventory. The challenge was that those goals could easily work against each other.

A more aggressive intervention might drive short-term conversion but also increase churn, customer support contacts, or reduce viewing hours that mattered to the ads business. From the outset, I treated this as a balancing problem, not a growth problem. Every design decision had to improve one metric without unintentionally harming another.

Driving alignment

Aligning the organization before building the system

Before the team designed a single screen, I recognized we needed alignment on what kind of intervention we were actually building. Product, Engineering, Legal, and the business each had different priorities and definitions of success, and discovery was happening in parallel without a shared view of the problem.

I led a three-day cross-functional workshop to synthesize what we had learned, surface the key tensions, and make the tradeoffs explicit. The outcome was alignment around a graduated intervention model and frameworks for where to introduce friction, where to avoid it, and how to protect legitimate subscribers.

Design principles

Designing for behavior change

From that workshop, I distilled the discussion into four experience principles. I deliberately chose principles over prescriptive rules because the work would continue to evolve. Product, Design, and Engineering needed a shared way to make tradeoffs without revisiting every decision from first principles.

Principle 01
Lead users forward, never force

Design every touchpoint to guide, not intimidate.

Principle 02
Design paths, not walls

Intervene with graduated friction based on context and behavior.

Principle 03
Preserve identity and continuity

Ensure users don't lose history, preferences, and maintain ownership.

Principle 04
Support users across surfaces

Support seamless movement between TV, mobile, and web.

The core tension

Disney's definition vs. how people define household

One of the most important outcomes of the workshop was recognizing a fundamental tension between the policy and the product promise. The policy defined household by primary residence, while the streaming experience had always promised flexibility: watch wherever you are, with your personalized experience following you.

Customers also brought their own definition of household, centered on family relationships rather than physical location. I challenged the team to design for that tension rather than designing around it. Instead of asking customers to abandon the value they had come to expect, we defined three core user needs that allowed us to introduce a new boundary while preserving the value of the product.

Cross-device conversion

People watch on TVs, but sign up on their phone

Another important constraint was the device itself. Much of the behavior we were trying to influence happened on TV, but TV is one of the most cumbersome places to create an account or complete a purchase.

The opportunity was recognizing that intent and completion didn't have to happen on the same device. TV is where the intent shows up, but mobile is often where the action can be completed with much less friction. I led the design of cross-device journeys that allowed users to move from TV to mobile and back without losing context.

From intervention to invitation

The first version felt punitive.
I redirected toward value, clarity, trust.

This was one of the moments where I got closest to the work. The first version technically solved the problem, but when I reviewed it, the experience felt too punitive. It led with policy instead of helping customers understand what was changing or what to do next.

I redirected the team toward an experience that felt more like an invitation than an intervention, leading with value and familiarity, making the choices clearer, and ensuring the experience felt simple and guided.

I also emphasized the importance of tone. It wasn't cosmetic. It was a strategic lever. It helped users understand what was changing, supported conversion, and protected the relationship.

Strategy

Designed a user behavior transition, not a feature.

Trade-offs

Balanced customer experience with revenue at a global scale.

Alignment

Aligned cross-functional teams under ambiguity.

Roadmap

What I'm most proud of wasn't a single feature. It was helping us shift from an enforcement mindset to a behavior-change model. The biggest lesson I carried forward: sustainable monetization starts with understanding people. When you design around customer behavior, not just business goals, you create experiences that drive lasting outcomes for both.
